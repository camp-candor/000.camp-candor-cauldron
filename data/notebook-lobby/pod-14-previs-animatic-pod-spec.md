## 1. Purpose

**Why this Pod exists as a distinct, bounded unit:** there's a massive cost asymmetry between a rough sketch and a final AI-rendered frame — generating a full high-resolution sequence only to discover the pacing doesn't work, a cut is confusing, or a shot doesn't read as intended is a direct waste of expensive GPU compute that a cheap rough pass could have caught first. If this function were skipped and Scene Pods went straight from a locked script to final rendering, every pacing mistake would only be discoverable at the most expensive possible point — after full-resolution generation — rather than at the cheapest. This Pod exists specifically to be the sandbox where that mistake is cheap.

**Single most important outcome this Pod is accountable for:** producing a locked animatic — a rough but timing-accurate story reel, cut to the locked audio track — that proves the visual pacing and shot structure work *before* any Scene Pod begins expensive high-resolution rendering.

**Explicitly outside this Pod's purpose:**
- This Pod does not produce final-quality frames — its output is deliberately low-fidelity; a rough sketch or basic 3D block is sufficient, since the goal is proving pacing works, not producing usable footage.
- This Pod does not write or revise the script — that's Narrative's job; Pre-Vis translates an already-locked script into visual blocking, it does not originate story content.
- This Pod does not lock character or environment visual design — that's Foundation's job; Pre-Vis uses Foundation's rough reference sheets for blocking purposes but does not itself decide what a character or environment definitively looks like.
- This Pod does not compose final music — that's Score & Composition's job, though Score uses this Pod's locked animatic as a spotting reference (see Section 4.2).

## 2. Method (Internal Operation)

**Step-by-step internal workflow:**
1. Receive the locked script and beat sheet from Narrative, and the locked master audio track from Audio (see Section 5.1) — both are required inputs, not optional references, since the animatic must be cut to the actual audio timing, not an estimate of it.
2. Receive rough visual reference sheets and lightweight style embeddings from Foundation, sufficient to block scenes using approved character silhouettes and environments rather than inventing placeholder designs that will be visually wrong later.
3. Storyboard/Pre-Vis Artist blocks out the scene shot by shot — camera angles, character positions, basic staging — using whichever rough method suits the scene (low-step AI generation, basic 3D blocking, or hand-sketched boards).
4. Editor/Timing Specialist cuts the rough blocking to the locked audio track, adjusting shot lengths and transitions until the pacing genuinely matches the audio's rhythm and silences.
5. Internal review: the Pod Lead checks the assembled rough reel against the Narrative beat sheet's intended structure — does this actually read as the scene was meant to, at this rough fidelity.
6. Submit the animatic to the Showrunner for the pod-to-Showrunner back-brief (Section 3.3) — this is the primary review gate for this Pod's output, given how directly downstream compute cost depends on getting this right before proceeding.
7. On approval, publish the locked animatic to the shared registry.
8. Notify downstream pods (via Infrastructure's event system) that a new locked animatic is available.

**Human roles inside the Pod and division of labor:**
- **Storyboard/Pre-Vis Artist** — the composition vector. Owns shot blocking, camera choices, and staging — translating the script's action into a sequence of visual beats.
- **Editor/Timing Specialist** — the pacing vector. Owns cutting that blocking to match the locked audio's actual rhythm, which is a different skill from composing individual shots.
- **Why split rather than combined:** blocking a shot well and cutting a sequence's pacing well draw on related but distinct judgment — a Pre-Vis Artist focused on making each individual shot's composition read clearly can lose track of whether the *sequence* of shots, cut to the real audio, actually paces correctly, which is exactly the kind of whole-versus-parts blind spot this split is designed to avoid (the same underlying logic as Assembly's Editor/Technical-Engineer split, applied one stage earlier in the pipeline).

**Tooling specifics:** the choice between low-step AI generation, basic 3D blocking, or hand-sketched boards is made per-scene based on what's fastest and clearest for that specific scene's needs — this Pod is deliberately tool-agnostic at this stage, since the entire point is speed and cheapness, not technical sophistication.

**Known failure modes of this Pod's own process, and internal catches:**
- *An animatic that reads fine shot-by-shot but doesn't actually prove out the scene's emotional pacing once assembled* — mitigated by step 5's explicit check against the Narrative beat sheet's intended structure, not just visual clarity of individual shots.
- *Blocking scenes using placeholder designs that don't match what Foundation will eventually lock*, causing the animatic's pacing proof to not actually transfer cleanly to final rendering — mitigated by requiring Foundation's rough reference sheets as an input (step 2) rather than allowing the Pod to invent its own placeholder designs.
- *Treating the animatic as disposable scratch work rather than a genuine boundary condition*, leading to a rushed pass that doesn't actually validate pacing — mitigated by the Showrunner back-brief (step 6) being a real, substantive review rather than a formality, given how much downstream compute cost depends on this Pod having done its job properly.

**Idle/blocked behavior:** This Pod fully blocks on a given scene until both the locked script/beat sheet (Narrative) and the locked audio track (Audio) are available — animating to an unlocked script risks the same wasted-effort problem described for Audio and Foundation, and animating without the real audio timing defeats the entire purpose of proving pacing against reality rather than an estimate. If Foundation's reference sheets are delayed but script and audio are ready, this Pod may begin blocking with clearly-marked placeholder designs, but must flag to the Showrunner that the animatic's visual fidelity (not its pacing) is provisional pending Foundation's assets.

## 3. Design Primitives — Explicit Mapping

### 3.1 Intent
The intent this Pod receives is typically framed around risk reduction rather than aesthetic direction: *"Prove that this scene's structure and pacing actually work before we commit expensive rendering to it — we'd rather find out now, cheaply, than after a Scene Pod has spent real compute on it."* Note this doesn't specify which blocking method to use, how detailed the boards need to be, or exactly how many revision passes are expected — the Pod is free to choose its own rough-visualization approach as long as the underlying risk-reduction guarantee holds.

### 3.2 Values as Boundary Conditions
- **Speed and cheapness over visual polish:** this Pod deliberately produces low-fidelity output — spending extra time making a rough board look more finished than it needs to be actively works against this Pod's purpose, since the value of this stage is specifically that mistakes here are cheap.
- **Real audio timing over estimated timing:** this Pod does not cut against a placeholder or estimated timing track — the entire risk-reduction value of the animatic depends on pacing being validated against the actual locked audio, not an approximation of it (Section 2, idle/blocked behavior).
- **Honest pacing proof over a flattering presentation:** if the rough cut reveals a genuine pacing problem, this Pod's job is to surface that clearly at the back-brief stage, not to polish or edit around the problem in a way that makes it less visible to the Showrunner — the whole point of this stage is catching problems here, so under-selling a real issue defeats the Pod's purpose.

### 3.3 Back-Brief
**Pod-to-Showrunner back-brief:** the locked animatic itself, submitted for the Showrunner's review before any Scene Pod begins rendering — described elsewhere in this framework as the review where the Showrunner validates narrative rhythm, pacing, and visual storytelling, and where structural story cuts happen specifically to avoid burning compute on a scene that doesn't work. This is this Pod's primary and highest-stakes review gate.

**Weaker-signal risk mitigation:** because this back-brief involves a human Showrunner reviewing a full assembled sequence (not just an isolated artifact), it sits closer to the stronger, explanation-capable end of the back-brief spectrum. The main residual risk is that the rough fidelity itself could mask a problem that would only be visible at higher resolution (e.g., a shot that reads fine as a rough block but doesn't actually work once real character performance is present) — this Pod does not claim to eliminate that risk entirely, only to catch the *structural and pacing* class of problems, which is the majority of what's expensive to discover late.

### 3.4 Global Read / Local Write
**Private working state:** in-progress boards, draft cuts, and rejected blocking attempts are visible only within the Pod while iteration is happening — no Scene Pod should be building against an unlocked, in-progress animatic.

**Finalized write:** once approved by the Showrunner, the Pod publishes the locked animatic once, immutably, to the shared registry, with a version tag.

**Read access:** Scene Pods, Assembly, and Score & Composition may all read the locked animatic freely.

**No downstream mutation:** confirmed — no downstream Pod may re-cut or alter the locked animatic directly. If a Scene Pod finds the blocking genuinely doesn't work once real rendering begins, that's escalated back to this Pod (or to the Showrunner) as a formal issue, not silently reinterpreted downstream.

### 3.5 Strict Contracts (Treat Pods like APIs)
**Formal input contract:** locked script and beat sheet (Narrative), locked master audio track (Audio), rough visual reference sheets and style embeddings (Foundation).

**Formal output contract:** one locked animatic video file, serving as the visual boundary condition for downstream Scene Pods.

**Contract change process:** if this Pod needs to revise a locked animatic after Scene Pods have already begun blocking their own work against it (e.g., a late structural note), the revision is published as a new version, with the Infrastructure Pod's event system notifying affected Scene Pods — any Scene Pod that has already started rendering against the prior version is flagged for review rather than assumed to remain valid, the same discipline established in Narrative's own contract-change process.

### 3.6 Event-Driven Triggers
**Trigger to begin work:** receipt of both a locked script/beat sheet from Narrative and a locked audio track from Audio (a joint trigger — this Pod does not begin full blocking work until both are present, per Section 2's idle/blocked behavior).

**Trigger this Pod's output causes downstream:** publishing a locked animatic fires an automated notification (via Infrastructure) to Scene Pods, Assembly, and Score & Composition that new locked visual-pacing material is available.

### 3.7 Black-Box Autonomy
While blocking and cutting are in progress, this Pod is not required to expose in-progress boards, rejected shot compositions, or draft timing adjustments to any other Pod. The only checkpoint visible outside the Pod is the Showrunner back-brief (Section 3.3) and, eventually, the final locked publication.

## 6. Risk and Mitigation

**Single biggest failure mode specific to this Pod:** an animatic passes the Showrunner back-brief at rough fidelity — the pacing reads fine as sketches or basic 3D blocks — but the same pacing doesn't actually hold once high-resolution character performance and real environmental detail are present, meaning the risk-reduction this Pod is supposed to provide silently fails to transfer to the stage it's meant to protect.

**Weaker-signal risk (framework-general, applied here):** this Pod's back-brief is a human review of an assembled sequence, closer to the stronger end of the back-brief spectrum described elsewhere in this framework — but it's still an artifact at a *different fidelity* than what it's meant to predict, which is its own distinct version of the general risk (not "AI versus human judgment," but "low-fidelity proxy versus high-fidelity reality"). **Mitigation used by this Pod:** requiring real audio timing (not estimated) and Foundation's actual rough references (not invented placeholders) as inputs, specifically to keep the animatic's proxy as close to reality as its low fidelity allows, minimizing (though not eliminating) the fidelity gap.

**Cost of an undetected error:** this Pod's entire purpose is cost avoidance, so a failure here has an unusual shape — it doesn't directly cost compute itself, but it *fails to prevent* the exact costly outcome (wasted high-resolution rendering on bad pacing) it exists to catch. If this Pod's back-brief passes something that later fails at Scene Pod fidelity, the cost is effectively the same as if this Pod hadn't existed for that scene at all — full re-render, at full expense, after the fact.

**Escalation path:** if a Scene Pod discovers, during high-resolution rendering, that the locked animatic's blocking genuinely doesn't work at real fidelity, this is escalated back to this Pod and the Showrunner jointly — treated as a signal to review whether the fidelity gap (Section 6) is a recurring pattern worth addressing in this Pod's process, not merely a one-off correction to that single scene.

## 7. Boundary Conditions and Scope Limits

- This Pod is explicitly forbidden from publishing a locked animatic without completing the Showrunner back-brief (Section 3.3) — given this Pod's entire purpose is risk reduction before expensive rendering, skipping this review defeats the Pod's reason for existing.
- This Pod may not cut an animatic against estimated or placeholder audio timing — real, locked audio is a hard input requirement (Section 3.2, Section 4.1), not a nice-to-have.
- This Pod does not have authority to make structural story changes on its own initiative if the rough cut reveals a pacing problem — that's flagged to the Showrunner and, if the fix requires content changes, routed back to Narrative, not resolved unilaterally within Pre-Vis.
- Default resource allocation: this Pod's compute needs are deliberately minimal (low-step generation or non-generative tools), reflecting its core value of being cheap — if this Pod's own workflow starts consuming compute comparable to a Scene Pod's, that's a signal the Pod has drifted from its intended low-fidelity purpose and should be reviewed.

## 8. Success Criteria

- The Showrunner knows this Pod succeeded when the locked animatic accurately predicts how a scene's final pacing will read, such that Scene Pods rarely if ever need to significantly deviate from its blocking once high-resolution rendering begins.
- A rejected animatic is returned to Pre-Vis with specific structural or pacing feedback; the Pod revises locally and resubmits for another back-brief round.
- **Contract success:** the animatic is delivered in the correct format, cut to the real locked audio track, on schedule.
- **Intent success:** the animatic's pacing proof actually holds at final Scene Pod fidelity — not just at the rough fidelity it was produced and reviewed at.
- **Concrete divergence case for this Pod:** an animatic could be contract-compliant (correctly formatted, cut to real audio, approved at back-brief) while still failing intent if its low-fidelity blocking simply didn't predict a problem that only emerges at high resolution — exactly the fidelity-gap risk in Section 6, and the reason this Pod's success is ultimately measured by how rarely Scene Pods need to deviate from it, not just by whether the back-brief was passed.

## 9. Open Questions / Notes

- The fidelity-gap risk (Section 6) is structurally unresolvable by this Pod alone — it's inherent to the trade-off of using a cheap, rough proxy to predict an expensive, detailed outcome. Worth tracking in practice how often Scene Pods actually do need to deviate significantly from locked animatics, since a high deviation rate would suggest this Pod's rough fidelity isn't actually sufficient to serve its risk-reduction purpose and may need to be raised (at some cost to the "cheap and fast" value in Section 3.2).
- **[Resolved]** The "significant deviation" escalation threshold is addressed by the cross-Pod escalation standard (see `cross_pod_escalation_threshold_standard.md`): three separate Scene Pod reports of the same animatic's blocking failing to hold at full rendering fidelity now triggers escalation, using a report-count unit rather than revision-attempt count, since a single scene is rarely re-blocked multiple times in isolation. The underlying fidelity-gap risk itself (Section 6) remains unresolved by this threshold — it defines when to escalate, not how to close the rough-versus-final fidelity gap.
- This Pod has strong, specific source material — including an entire dedicated passage listing its exact upstream/downstream/parallel relationships — so Section 4 here draws especially directly and faithfully from that material rather than being a heavier extrapolation; this is one of the better-grounded specs in the set alongside Foundation, Audio, and Infrastructure.
