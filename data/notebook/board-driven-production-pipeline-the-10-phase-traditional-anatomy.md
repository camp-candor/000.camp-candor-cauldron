# Board-Driven Production Pipeline: The 10-Phase Traditional Anatomy

A structural breakdown of the visual-first, board-driven production pipeline pioneered on *Samurai Jack*, contrasting text-heavy screenwriting with visual-first storytelling.

---

## 1. The Core Philosophy: Replacing the Script with the Board

Traditional television animation relies on text scripts written by drama or comedy writers. In visual-first action animation, text scripts fail because a single line—*"Jack fights three robots in the snow"*—cannot communicate choreography, aspect-ratio shifts, silence durations, or spatial dynamics that occupy seven minutes of screen time.

Instead of a full script, the process relies on a **2-to-3-page outline** detailing emotional beats, environmental atmosphere, sensory cues, and combat pacing. The storyboard artist becomes the de facto writer, director, and pacing architect of the episode.

---

## 2. The 10 Production Phases

```
[ 1. Outline & Thumbnail ] ──> [ 2. Storyboarding & Pitch ] ──> [ 3. Art Direction & Color Scripts ]
                                                                             │
[ 6. Animatic / Leica Reel ] <── [ 5. Voice Recording ] <── [ 4. Character & Prop Models ]
              │
              └──> [ 7. Animation Production (Keys/Inbetweens/Digital Ink & Paint) ]
                                            │
[ 10. Final Stem Mix & Delivery ] <── [ 9. Score (DJ Hybrid) ] <── [ 8. Post Editing & Foley ]
```

### Phase 1: Premise & Thumbnail Outline
* **Team:** Showrunner, Story Supervisor, Key Board Artists.
* **Deliverable:** 2–3 page outline emphasizing emotional temperature, key combat beats, and sensory dynamics rather than dialogue.

### Phase 2: Storyboarding ("Writing with Pictures")
* **Timeline:** 5–6 weeks per 22-minute episode (approx. 300–500 drawings).
* **Scope:** Dictates camera angles (extreme wides, Dutch angles, eye close-ups), pacing holds, split-screen paneling, and minimal dialogue.
* **The Pitch:** Artists pitch boards on physical corkboards, acting out character voices, sound effects (`swish`, `clang`, `boom`), and camera pans.

### Phase 3: Art Direction & Background Design (Lineless Aesthetic)
* **Inspiration:** UPA graphic style and Eyvind Earle (*Sleeping Beauty*).
* **Layouts:** Storyboard sketches expanded into precise architectural/organic scale layouts.
* **Color Scripts:** Small conceptual color sequences mapping emotional color arcs (e.g., despair in desaturated blues; combat tension in sharp reds and deep ambers).
* **Painting:** Bold, textured gouache/acrylic or digital paintings without black outlines, separating foreground figures from backgrounds via contrast and color theory alone.

### Phase 4: Character & Prop Design (Silhouette-Driven)
* **Design Philosophy:** Primitive geometric shapes (triangles for hostility/danger; circles for friendliness/stability).
* **Silhouette Test:** Character forms must be instantly recognizable when filled with pure black.
* **Deliverables:** Turnarounds, facial expression sheets, and prop sheets.

### Phase 5: Voice Recording
* **Sequencing:** Recorded *after* storyboards are mostly locked, allowing actors to view visual staging and frame timing.
* **Performance Contrast:** Minimalist, grounded subtlety (e.g., Phil LaMarr's stoic delivery) set against theatrical, eccentric antagonism (e.g., Mako's bombastic Aku).

### Phase 6: Animatic / Leica Reel (Timing Lock)
* **Tooling:** Editorial NLE (Avid/Premiere).
* **Process:** Storyboard panels conformed to recorded voices, temp sound effects, and temp music (classic samurai scores, spaghetti westerns, ambient drone).
* **Pacing Lock:** The Showrunner determines frame-accurate cut points. Becomes the non-negotiable temporal blueprint for downstream animators.

### Phase 7: Animation Production (Key Animation & Digital Paint)
* **Facility:** Traditional overseas animation houses (e.g., Rough Draft Studios).
* **Key Animation:** Senior animators draw extremes ("keys") defining mass, momentum, and follow-through.
* **Inbetweening:** Alternating between animating "on ones" (24 fps) for fast martial arts impact and "on twos/threes" (12/8 fps) for stylized holds.
* **Clean-up & Digital Paint:** Pencil lines inked and colored to match fills, creating the signature lineless look before compositing over painted backgrounds.

### Phase 8: Post-Production Editing & Foley
* **Visual Conform:** Color takes replace black-and-white animatic panels; retakes issued for timing or color errors.
* **Foley & Sound Design:** Sound carries the primary narrative weight. Foley artists record practical footsteps, metal scrapes, and hydraulic thuds, juxtaposing stylized cartoon effects with raw cinematic realism.

### Phase 9: Musical Score (Hybrid Composition)
* **Philosophy:** Scoring like a DJ rather than continuous orchestral underscore.
* **Instrumentation:** Blending traditional instruments (taiko, shakuhachi, shamisen) with heavy synthesizers, industrial techno, and hip-hop beats.
* **Strategic Silence:** Combat scenes frequently strip away all music to let the clash of steel and human breathing command the scene.

### Phase 10: The Final Mix & Stem Delivery
* **Mixing Stage:** Showrunner, sound designers, and mixers balance four separated audio stems: Dialogue (DX), Foley, Sound Effects (FX), and Music (MX).
* **Dynamics:** Precision balancing between deafening kinetic explosions and drop-to-zero dead air right before a killing blow.
* **Delivery:** Master conform and broadcast tape output.
